Why Do Small Cap Stocks Fade After the Morning Spike?

A small cap stock usually fades after the morning spike because new supply arrives: the company sells fresh shares into the rally, and holders of its warrants and notes sell alongside it. The news that started the run is rarely what ends it.

Marcus T. · Wed Nov 11

The Fade Was Not About the News

Picture a biotech with five million shares in its float. At 7 AM it posts trial results that are genuinely good. By the open it is up 70% on more volume than it trades in a typical month, and inside ten minutes it halts on the way up.

It reopens higher. You buy the first pullback and get paid. You buy the second one and get paid again.

Then the chart changes character. The stock trades its heaviest volume of the day and cannot make a new high. Every push toward the morning peak meets an offer that refills at the same price. No headline explains it yet.

At 10:40 AM one does. The company has priced a registered direct offering, sold to a handful of funds under the market price, with warrants attached. By lunch the stock has given back two thirds of the run. The trial data never changed.

The usual explanations are profit taking, bears piling in, sentiment turning. None of them fits a stock that gives back its gains on the biggest volume of the session. A trader betting against a stock can only sell shares that already exist. The company can create new ones, and it just did.

The rule to carry into tomorrow: when a stock with real news stalls on its heaviest volume, assume a seller with fresh shares until a filing proves otherwise.

What Is a Small-Cap in the Stock Market?

The textbook answer is a company valued at roughly $300 million to $2 billion. The answer that pays at the open is different: a small cap is usually a company that spends more cash than it brings in, and covers the gap by selling stock.

That makes the company a trader in its own shares, with one goal. It wants the highest price it can get for the stock it sells, and the best price it will see all quarter is the morning its chart goes vertical. Your breakout is its pricing window.

The float is not fixed, either. Authorized shares are the ceiling the charter allows, shares outstanding are what has been issued, and the float is the slice that trades freely. A company can climb from 20 million shares to 120 million in two years, and every rally along the way was met by new stock. Last year's clean chart was drawn with a share count that no longer exists.

Thin floats make the arithmetic harsh. A registered direct of three million shares into a five-million-share float adds 60% more tradable stock in a single afternoon. Those buyers paid a discount, so they can sell into your bid at a profit as soon as the deal closes.

Before you trade a gapper, know three numbers: how many shares float, how many months of cash the company has left, and whether it already has a registered way to sell stock.

How New Shares Reach the Tape

New supply arrives in a few recognizable shapes, and each one leaves a different mark.

  • The shelf. An S-3 registration lets a company sell stock later without starting the process over. On EDGAR, an EFFECT notice means the SEC has declared it effective, and from that day the channel is open. A shelf on its own is not a sale, but it means a sale can happen any morning the price suits the company.
  • The registered direct. Shares sold to a few funds off the shelf, priced under the market, often announced the same day the stock spikes. The prospectus supplement appears on EDGAR as a 424B5. Because the shares are registered, the buyers can sell them right away, which is why the drop comes fast.
  • The at-the-market program. A sales agent feeds shares into the market at the going price, a little at a time, with no headline on the day. You learn how much was sold when the next quarterly report comes out. On the tape it looks like a seller who never runs out.
  • Units, warrants and convertible notes. A unit pairs each share with a warrant, so the deal sells twice: the shares now, and the warrant shares later once the price clears the strike. Warrants and notes already outstanding work the same way. Their owners sell or hedge into every rally, which is how a stock can absorb real news and go nowhere.

You rarely see the filing before the fade starts. You see the tape, and it tells you first:

  • Heavy volume with no new high. Buyers keep showing up, and someone with more stock keeps meeting them.
  • An offer that reloads at one price after every print.
  • A halt on the way up that reopens weak. The chasers who waited for the reopen found no one to sell to.
  • The float trading over several times by mid-morning while the price sits under the high. The eager buyers have already bought.

Why You Buy the Fourth Dip Anyway

Knowing all of this does not prevent the loss, because the loss is not a knowledge problem.

The news anchors you. The data was good at 7 AM and it is still good, so a red candle reads like a gift. You bought three dips on the way up and each one paid, so the fourth one feels identical, even though the seller on the other side has changed.

Then the offering crosses, and it reads like noise. The funds bought in, you tell yourself, so they believe in the stock. The warrants carry a higher strike, so someone will defend that price. Both stories ask you to trust the one party in the trade whose job today is to sell you stock.

So you buy more under your first entry, and the chart is the only research you did. The shelf that made the offering possible was public for months before you clicked buy.

Each of those steps feels like trading. Together they are a pattern, and it repeats every time a cash-poor company gets a good headline. When you catch yourself explaining why an offering is good news, close that name's order window for the day.

What Is the Most Successful Day Trading Strategy?

No setup works on a stock the company is selling into. The strategy that lasts on small caps is the one you still follow at 10:40 AM, when the offering crosses and the news still looks good. Three rules make it concrete:

  • Read the supply before the open. For every gapper on your list, check whether a shelf is effective, whether an at-the-market program is open, how many months of cash are left and where the warrants sit. An open channel plus a thin cash cushion means the spike is the company's window as much as yours.   • When an offering crosses, the trade is over for buyers. Write it as a rule before the open: no new buys in a name once its offering is announced, however good the news.
  • Treat heavy volume with no new high as a reason to sell, never to add. Take profit while the buyers are still lifting offers.

So why do small caps fade once the morning spike prints? Because the company that issued the stock, and everyone owning its warrants and notes, was waiting for exactly that spike to sell.
