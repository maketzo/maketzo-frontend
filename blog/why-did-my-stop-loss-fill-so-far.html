STOP LOSSES

Why Did My Stop Loss Fill So Far Below My Price?

A stop loss fills under your stop price because a stop is a trigger, not a price: the moment the stock reaches your level, your broker sends a market order, and that order sells to whatever bids are left. On a thin small cap those bids can sit 10, 20 or 50 cents under your stop, and they thin out at the exact moment every other stop fires.

Ludy D. · Thu Nov 19

Your Stop Picks the Moment. The Bids Pick the Price.

A stop-market order has one job: wait for a price, then sell at the market.

It is 10:04. A trader is long 1,000 shares of a made-up $4.80 stock that ran on news at the open; the numbers are for illustration, not a suggested position. The morning pullback bottomed at $4.52, so the stop sits at $4.50, a clean half dollar under the low. On Level 2, the bids under that stop read 600 shares at $4.49, 300 at $4.47, 400 at $4.45, 500 at $4.41, then nothing until $4.31.

Read that stack the way the stop will. When the stock reaches $4.50, the stop stops being a stop. It becomes a market order, and a market order never asks what the shares are worth. It takes the best bid, then the next, then the next, until the shares are sold or the bids run out. The trader's 1,000 shares are more than the first two bids can take, so part of the exit lands at $4.45 or worse before anything else goes wrong.

Treat that stack as a snapshot, not a promise. Displayed bids can be pulled in the time it takes to click, new bids can arrive, and some size never shows on the screen at all. The stack tells you roughly how much is there right now, not what you will get. What sets the stop off also varies: some brokers trigger on the last sale, others on the bid or the quote, and on a thin stock those can sit cents apart.

On a large cap that walk is invisible, because thousands of shares sit at every penny. On a small cap it can cost more than the trade was ever going to make.

  • Before the entry, read the bids under your stop, not just the stop.
  • Add up the shares bid within a few cents of it, and assume the total can shrink before your order arrives.
  • If that stack is thin, expect the fill to land under the price you typed.
  • Ask your broker whether its stops trigger on the last sale or on the quote.

Every Stop in the Room Sits at the Same Price

The obvious level is where the bids disappear.

The trader did not pick $4.50 alone. Anyone who bought the same pullback saw the same $4.52 low and the same round half dollar, so a crowd of stops is parked in the same few cents. At 10:05 the stock ticks to $4.50 and they all fire together. The 600 at $4.49 goes to whichever order reaches the exchange first. The bidders at $4.47 and $4.45 can see the selling coming and pull before it lands. By the time the trader's order arrives, the best bid left is $4.41. Half the order fills there and the other half at $4.31.

Average fill: $4.36. That is 14 cents under the stop, on a stock whose bid and offer sat two cents apart a minute earlier.

This is why the worst fill of the morning so often comes at the most obvious level. Morning lows, premarket highs, whole and half dollars: everyone can see them, so everyone's exit is parked there, and the bidders know it.

  • Mark the obvious levels near your stop before the open.
  • If your stop sits on one, plan the trade around the fill you will likely get, not the price you typed.
  • Some traders sell by hand when the bid starts thinning ahead of the level. If that is your exit, decide it before the entry, not while the bids are leaving.

Your Planned Risk Is Not Your Actual Risk

A stop loss controls your decision to exit. It does not fully control how much you lose.

Put the trader's numbers side by side: - Entry: $4.80 - Planned stop: $4.50 - Planned loss on 1,000 shares: $300 - Average fill: $4.36 - Actual loss: $440 - Added by slippage: $140, or 46.7% more than the plan

The trader did everything right. The stop went in before the entry, it was never moved, and it fired on time. The loss was still 46.7% bigger than planned. That is not a discipline failure. It is the gap between risk, which you choose, and execution quality, which the book decides for you. Mixing the two up leads traders to blame themselves for a fill, or to forgive themselves for a moved stop.

So plan from the fill, not from the stop: - Measure a trade's risk from the price you expect to get, not the price you typed. If a realistic fill under a $4.50 stop is $4.36, the trade risks 44 cents a share, not 30, and that is the number your position has to fit inside. - Build the allowance from what you can see: the spread, the bids within a few cents of the stop, and whether the stop sits on a level everyone else is using. The thinner each one looks, the wider the allowance. - If the realistic fill makes the trade bigger than your plan allows, the trade does not fit. Passing is a complete answer.

Liquidity Sets the Fill, Not Market Cap

The market cap tells you what a company is worth. Liquidity tells you what your exit is worth.

A small cap is usually a company worth roughly $300 million to $2 billion, with microcaps under that, and on a day trader's screen the word covers both. None of those numbers says where your stop will fill. A $900 million biotech with 5 million shares in the float can carry a book as thin as a $40 million name, because depth comes from the float and how many shares trade, not from what the company is worth.

The spread moves with the depth. A stock quoted two cents wide at 9:45 can be 25 cents wide in a flush, and a stop that fires in that moment sells into the low side of the gap. Depth also changes through the day: a stop that would fill cleanly in a busy first hour can slip far more in the midday lull, when fewer shares are bid at each price.

Slippage is the exit half of a bigger question, who is left to buy from you, and the guide to small cap day trading follows one trade from the float to the last buyer.

  • Judge a stop by the float, the average volume and the bids near your level, never by the market cap.
  • Check the spread before the entry. If it is wide while the stock is calm, it will be wider while it falls.
  • Read the book at the moment you enter, not the one you saw at 9:45.

When a Stop Cannot Fire

A stop only triggers while the stock is trading, and small caps stop trading often.

There are three times your stop can do nothing: - A halt. When a stock moves too fast through its price bands, trading pauses, usually for at least five minutes, and a stop cannot trigger while the stock is frozen. If the stock reopens under your stop, the stop triggers once trading resumes and sells into whatever bids exist then. That is not necessarily the reopening price: the fill can land at it, past it, or in pieces, depending on how your broker handles stops around a halt. The guide to trading halts covers the codes and what orders do while a stock is frozen. - Before 9:30 and after 4:00. Brokers differ on which order types they accept outside regular hours and on whether a stop triggers there at all. At many, a standard stop waits for the open, so a stock that drops through it at 7:50 can open far lower. Some offer extended-hours stops that become limit orders when triggered, which a fast stock can run straight past. DAS Trader Pro's LimitP works that way, and the LimitP primer shows where it helps and where it fails. - Overnight. A small cap that announces an offering after the close can open under every stop on the board. A stop never protects you from a gap. It only sells after one.

Ask your broker exactly what your stop does in a halt and outside 9:30 to 4:00 before you hold a position through either. The overnight gap is the one you can see coming: most small caps burn cash and sell stock into strength, and their SEC filings show whether a company can sell shares right now and how recently it has. Read them before you hold a small cap past the close.

Stop-Market, Stop-Limit, or Your Own Hand

Every exit order trades one risk for another, so pick the risk before you send it.

  • A stop-market puts getting out ahead of the price. Once triggered it becomes a market order, which usually fills fast, but nothing guarantees an immediate or complete fill: in a halt, or with the bids gone, part of it can wait or land far lower. That is the order that filled at $4.36.
  • A stop-limit sets the worst price you will take. If the stock gaps through your limit, the order sits unfilled and you still own a falling stock.
  • A sell limit a few cents under the bid, sent by hand or by hotkey, takes the bids that are there down to your floor and stops. It is fast and it caps the damage, but only if you are watching and willing to press it.

None of them makes the slip go away. The slip is the cost of trading a thin stock. The expensive mistake comes after it: the stock bounces to $4.70 by 10:20, the trader decides the stop was wrong, and the next stop goes in wider, or not at all. If fills keep landing past your stops, change the plan before the next entry, with a different level, a stock with more bids, or no trade, and leave the stop alone once you are in.

So why did a stop at $4.50 fill at $4.36? Because the stop only chose the moment. The price came from the bids still standing when every stop in the room fired at once, and the ones the trader reached averaged 14 cents under $4.50. The stop controlled the decision to exit. The book decided what it cost.
