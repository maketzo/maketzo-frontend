What Does the Zella Score Actually Measure?

The Zella Score measures what your trades produced: TradeZella builds it from six results, and profit factor, average win against average loss and maximum drawdown carry most of the weight. None of the six asks whether you followed your plan, so a week of broken rules that ends green can grade better than a week you traded clean.

John P. · Wed Nov 11

Two Weeks, One Score

Picture two weeks of your own small cap trading.

In the first week you trade the plan. You take only the gappers on your list, buy the first clean pullback, cut each loser at the price you wrote down before the open, and walk away after your second loss of the day because your rules say so. Ten trades, four winners, and the week ends a little red.

In the second week you trade like someone else. Monday's first trade loses, so you jump straight back in to win it back, and lose again. Wednesday you blow through your daily loss limit and keep going. Thursday you chase a low-float runner with no plan, minutes before it halts on the way up, and it reopens 30% higher. That one trade pays for the whole week and then some. The week ends green.

Now run both weeks through a score built from results. The two inputs that carry the most weight in the Zella Score, profit factor and the ratio of your average win to your average loss, make up 45% of it, and both favor the second week. One outsized winner lifts the average win and pushes profit factor well past 1. The first week's profit factor sits under 1, because the week ended red, and every red week does.

The score has just ranked the week you broke four rules over the week you kept every one of them.

Before you trust any score at the end of a week, ask which of those two weeks it would have ranked higher. If the answer is the second, the number is grading your luck along with your trading.

What Is a Good Zella Score?

TradeZella's help center describes the Zella Score as "a single, actionable metric to assess and improve your trading performance." Each of six inputs is scored from 0 to 100, weighted, and combined:

  • Profit factor, 25%
  • Average win/loss ratio, 20%
  • Maximum drawdown, 20%
  • Win rate, 15%
  • Recovery factor, 10%
  • Consistency, 10%

On its own terms the design is sound. Drawdown and consistency punish wild swings, so a trader who blows up days in a row does pay for it. A good Zella Score means the shape of your results was good: winners bigger than losers, shallow drawdowns, steady days.

What it cannot tell you is how you got there. All six inputs are computed from trade results, so the score sees the money and never the decision. A small cap trader can post a fine profit factor for a month off two runners caught while breaking the daily loss limit, and the score will read that month as skill.

TradeZella does give rules a home of their own: a daily checklist in its Progress Tracker, and tags for rules followed or broken. Those sit beside the Zella Score, not inside it. Its mistake tags track the dollar impact of each mistake, which carries the same blind spot, because a revenge trade that happened to work shows up as money made.

The rule of thumb: read any results score as a description of what the market paid you, never as a grade on how you traded.

Why a Results Score Trains the Wrong Habit

A results score does more than mismeasure the week. It teaches you to repeat the wrong one.

In 1988 the psychologists Jonathan Baron and John Hershey gave people decisions that others had made under uncertainty, including monetary gambles, along with everything the decision maker had known at the time. People rated the same thinking as better when it turned out well than when it turned out badly.[1] Same information, same choice, a different grade, decided by the outcome.

A trader who grades the day by its P&L runs that same experiment every night. Thursday's runner is the trade you remember. The score agreed it was a good week. So the next Monday, when the first trade loses, the urge to jump back in arrives with evidence behind it: it worked last time. That is how a broken rule becomes a habit. It gets paid once, rated well, and tried again with more conviction, until the runner you chase is the one that halts and reopens 40% lower.

The fix at the open is simple and uncomfortable. Grade each decision before you look at what it paid. If you see the money first, the money writes the grade.

What Grading the Process Looks Like at the Open

Process grading only works if the standard exists before the first trade. Three rules make it concrete:

  • Write the rules while you are calm. Before the bell: which setups you will take, your daily loss limit, and the point at which you quit for the day. A rule written at 10:15 AM, after two losses, is a rationalization.
  • Grade each trade against a rule, not a result. Followed, broke, or it did not apply. A green trade that broke a rule is marked broken, and a red one that followed every rule is marked followed.
  • Score the day before you open the P&L. The money will still be there. It just should not get the first word.

The biggest part of it is whether you kept your rules and the contract you signed that morning: your strikes, your ceiling and how the day ended. The first week, the one where you kept every rule, lands near the top of the scale, which MAKETZO calls Ran It Clean. The second week's Thursday reads Broke the Plan, however green the screen was.

Is TradeZella Worth It for a Small Cap Trader?

It depends on the job you need done. TradeZella is strong at what it was built for: imports from many brokers, deep analytics on your results, replay and backtesting. If your problem is that you do not know your numbers, that is real help.

If your problem is that you know your rules and break them under pressure, a number computed from your P&L will keep telling you the wrong week was the good one. That trader needs the nightly grade to sit on the rules themselves. The full side-by-side for that choice, features and imports included, is in our tradezella alternative comparison.

So what does the Zella Score actually measure? It measures the results of your trades: profit factor, how your winners compare with your losers, drawdown, win rate, recovery and day-to-day consistency. It measures what happened, never whether you did what you said you would.

Before your next open, take last week's trades and mark each one followed or broke against the rules you had written. Then lay that list next to the P&L. Every trade where the two disagree is a habit your current score is paying you to keep.
