TRADING HALTS

What Happens When a Stock You Own Halts Down?

When your stock halts down, trading stops for at least five minutes, your stop cannot fire, and the price it comes back at is set by an auction rather than by the last trade. Every sell order that piles up during the pause gets priced in that auction at once, which is why a halt down rarely ends the selling: it stores it.

Jeremy B. · Thu Nov 19

A Halt Down Stores the Selling

The band decides where the stock stops. The sellers decide where it reopens.

It is 10:41. A trader is long a made-up small cap at $4.90. It closed yesterday at $2.60, so it trades with a 20% band, and with its five-minute average at $5.00, the floor of that band sits at $4.00. There is no news. A seller hits the bid, the stops under the morning low start firing, and in 40 seconds the stock drops from $4.85 to $4.00. The trader's stop-limit fires at $4.40 on the way down, and its $4.35 limit never fills, because the bids were already gone.

At $4.00 the best offer sits on the lower band. Nobody can sell under it, and after 15 seconds the exchange calls a five-minute pause. The screen goes still.

This is the part most traders get wrong. The stock stopped at $4.00 because the rules would not let it trade lower, not because the selling ran out. Everyone who wanted out under $4.00 is still there, and so is everyone who decides in the next five minutes that they want out too. All of those orders wait for the reopening auction.

  • Read a halt down as selling on hold, not selling finished.
  • Expect your stop to do nothing until the stock trades again. The first print after the reopen can set it off, wherever that print lands.
  • Write down the halt price and the time the moment the screen freezes. Every decision in the next few minutes starts from those two numbers.

Read the Code, Then the Indicative Price

The code tells you what kind of wait this is. The indicative price tells you how far the stock is going.

Check the halt code before anything else. A volatility pause shows as LUDP, lasts five minutes at the minimum, and the stock trades again the same day. A T1 means the company has news on the way, and then the next print depends on what the company says, not on the band. A T12 means the exchange wants answers from the company, and there is no clock on that one at all. The guide to halts lists every code you will meet and how long each one tends to last.

On a volatility pause, the number that matters next is the indicative price, the level the auction would clear at right now. On a Nasdaq listing, the first auction can price only so far under the band where the stock froze, 5% under it, or 15 cents when that band is $3 or less. If the indicative price sits under that limit, the pause extends by five minutes and the limit drops another step.

At 10:42 the indicative price on the trader's stock reads $3.65, and the limit has to step down twice to reach it: - 10:41 to 10:46. The auction can price no lower than $3.80, so it cannot clear at $3.65. The pause extends. - 10:46 to 10:51. The limit drops to $3.60. The indicative price now sits inside it, so the auction can clear when this window ends. Past ten minutes, a stuck auction can release the stock at any second, not only on the five-minute marks.

That gap, $4.00 to $3.65, is the whole story of the wait. A 9% gap is about two steps, so plan on roughly ten minutes. A 20% gap is four steps and closer to twenty, if the indicative price holds still, which it rarely does.

Your Market Order Keeps It Frozen

The instinct in a halt down is to send a market sell. That order is one of the reasons the stock is still frozen.

A market order sent into the pause takes whatever price the auction sets, and a pile of market orders the auction cannot fill is one of the two things that extends the wait. So the trader who panics into a market sell gets the worst of both: no control over the price, and a longer freeze.

What you can do depends on your broker and the route. Some routes let you place, change or cancel an order during a halt and queue it into the reopening auction. Others reject it or hold it until the stock trades again. Find out which yours does on a quiet day, not with a frozen position.

  • If your route queues orders into the auction, a sell limit at the worst price you will accept is a way to line up an exit, with no promise of a full fill or a good one.
  • Look at any order you left resting before the halt. The trader's $4.35 limit sell is still sitting there, and it will not fill in an auction priced at $3.65.
  • Do not buy more while the stock is halted. Averaging down into an auction you do not control is a trade you cannot manage until someone else sets the price. Thin stocks punish that habit faster than any other, which is the point of why a low float is a room with no exits.

The Reopen Is Not the Bottom

The first print after a halt down is a price, not a verdict.

At 10:51 the auction clears and the trader's stock reopens at $3.62. New bands form around the reopen, so the stock can freeze again if the selling keeps coming. It can also bounce hard, because some of the orders in that auction came from traders who sold in a panic and will buy back the first green minute. Neither move tells you the stock is safe.

The reopen is where most of the damage gets locked in, and where a second mistake usually starts. A stock that just halted down and reopened 10% lower looks cheap, and the bounce looks like a chance to make the money back. That buy has nothing behind it but the wish to undo the last ten minutes. Passing on it is the trade, and the hardest trade is the one you never take.

  • Give the reopen a few minutes before you judge it. The first print is one auction, not a settled price.
  • If you still own shares, act on the plan you set during the pause, not on the first candle.
  • If you are flat, a halt down is not a dip to buy. Let the stock show you a price it can hold.

So what happened to the stock the trader owned when it halted down? The selling did not stop at $4.00. It stopped being allowed to trade, waited ten minutes in a growing pile, and the auction priced all of it at once at $3.62.
