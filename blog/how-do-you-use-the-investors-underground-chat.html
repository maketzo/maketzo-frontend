CHAT ROOMS

How Do You Use the Investors Underground Chat Without Chasing?

You use the Investors Underground chat without chasing by taking the idea and leaving the entry: a call tells you where to look, and your own plan decides whether there is still a trade by the time you get there. Once the stock has moved away from the price in the call, stop judging the caller's trade and start judging whether a new setup exists, because if there is no new structure, there is no new trade.

John P. · Thu Nov 19

Take the Idea, Not the Entry

A call in the chat is the end of someone else's homework and the start of yours.

It is 9:41. A trader in the Investors Underground chat posts a made-up small cap breaking its premarket high of $3.10 on a fresh headline. Within a minute the room is full of questions about where the stop goes, and the stock is $3.38. A member who has spent the morning on a different name sees the call, opens the chart and buys at $3.40.

That member knows not to chase, and so does nearly everyone reading the call. Traders chase anyway, because a call borrows someone else's conviction and makes it feel like their own plan. A trader they respect saw something, the room agrees, the price is moving, and sitting still feels like the only mistake left. The chase feels like following a plan. It is following somebody else's plan, minus the stop, the exit and the fill that made it work.

Investors Underground is built for exactly this kind of speed. Its home page promises "real-time alerts and trade ideas in the chat room," and a member review on the same page sets out what the room is for: the goal of the community "is not to give you signals but to share the concepts so you can recognize the patterns in order to make your own trading decisions."

  • When a call lands, note the price and the time it was posted.
  • Open your own chart before you read the next message.
  • If the name was not on your list this morning, it starts as something to watch, not something to buy.

You're Not Trading Their Setup. You're Trading Their Aftermath.

The price in the call is not your entry. The moment the stock leaves that level, the caller's trade is over for you, and the only question is whether a new one has formed.

Lay the 9:41 trade out level by level:

  • $3.10: the premarket high, and the breakout the call was about.
  • $2.95: where that breakout idea is wrong, 15 cents under the entry. - $3.40: the member's fill, 30 cents after the call.
  • $3.50: the next supply, yesterday's high and a round half dollar.

From $3.40, the old stop at $2.95 means 45 cents of risk, three times what the call was built on. Pull the stop up to $3.25 to keep it at 15 cents, and it sits in the middle of a fresh spike, where an ordinary wiggle takes it out. Either way, the reward is 10 cents to $3.50. A 15 cent stop is no bargain when the stock has a dime of room before it runs into sellers.

None of that means the stock is finished. By 9:52 it has pulled back to $3.27, held $3.22 twice and started to curl up. That is a new setup with its own numbers: an entry near $3.30, wrong under $3.21, 20 cents of room to $3.50. It might be worth taking and it might not, but it can be judged on its own structure, which the 9:41 chase never could.

  • Stop measuring against the call price once the stock has left it.
  • Look for a new level: a pullback that holds, a higher low, a tight range near the high.
  • Price the trade both ways before you click: the distance to where you are wrong, and the distance to the next sellers.
  • If there is no new structure, there is no new trade. Waiting for one costs nothing.

Use the Room for Discovery and Your Plan for Execution

If you cannot explain a trade without quoting someone in the chat, you are holding a borrowed opinion with a stop attached.

Investors Underground gives you more to work with than the chat. Its daily game plan runs on "nightly watch lists" and a pre-market broadcast, and its tools include market scanners, news alerts and Top Ticker Lists. Used in order, they make a workflow: - Before the open, work the watch list and the broadcast into a short list of names, each with the level that gets you in and the level that proves you wrong. - During the session, read the chat as a discovery feed. A new name in the room is a reason to open a chart, not to send an order. - Before any entry, check six things yourself: the catalyst, the liquidity (float, volume and spread), the structure, whether the company's filings let it sell shares into the move, where you are wrong, and how far it is to the next sellers. - After a fast move, wait for a fresh setup instead of trying to copy someone's fill. - After the close, sort your trades by where each idea came from.

The company's write-up of an interview with Nathan Michaud, who founded Investors Underground and writes as InvestorsLive, draws the line where a chat member needs it: "It's okay to be inspired by other traders but you shouldn't be influenced by them." It adds a sentence worth keeping next to the chat window: "One thing that all great traders have in common is that they think for themselves."

Find Out Whether the Chat Is Helping You or Costing You

If you never sort your trades by where the idea came from, you never learn what the room is worth to you.

Michaud's trading psychology tips give the reason to keep count: "You are not going to catch every trade." The goal, the tip goes on, is to "focus on the opportunities where you have an edge." To find yours, tag every trade with where the idea came from, your own plan or the chat, and record more than the money: - The setup, and whether the entry came at your level or after the move. - Your planned risk and the risk you actually took. - The result in R, which is the result divided by the risk you planned. - Whether you kept every rule you set that morning.

Give it time. A week of trades is too few to prove an edge, so keep the tally until each pile holds enough trades to mean something. And keep money and process in separate columns. A chase that made $500 and a clean setup that lost $100: by the money, the chase wins. By the process, the chase is the trade that teaches you to chase again, and the clean loss is the one you want to repeat. Another tip on the same list keeps the comparison honest: "Learn from other traders and get inspired by other traders, but DON'T compare yourself."

So how do you use the Investors Underground chat without chasing? Not the way the member did at 9:41. Note the price at the call, stop judging the caller's trade once the stock leaves it, and buy only a setup you can price yourself, from where you are wrong to where the sellers wait. The room handed that member a good idea at $3.10 and a fair second chance at $3.30. The chase at $3.40 was the member's own.
