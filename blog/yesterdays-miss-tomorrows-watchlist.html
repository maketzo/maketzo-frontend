FOMO

Yesterday's miss writes tomorrow's watchlist

Fear of missing out doesn't stop at the chase: the night after a miss is often when your scanner and your idea of a setup quietly change.

Ludy D. · Wed Nov 25

Which filter on your scanner came from a stock you didn't trade? It's a harder question than it sounds. Most scanners don't keep a note saying when a filter was added or why, and the filters that came from a miss rarely feel like they did. They feel like pattern recognition.

THE OVERNIGHT EDIT

What changes between the close and the open

Here is a scenario a lot of small cap traders will recognize. A trader's scanner looks for stocks priced $1 to $3 with news that morning. A low float opens at $4 and runs to $11, and it never appears on her scan, because it opened a dollar above her price filter. She first sees it in a chat room at $8, too far from any entry her rules allow, so she doesn't chase.

Not chasing is the part FOMO advice usually covers. The part it skips happens that night.

By 8pm she's still replaying the chart. By 11pm she's scrolling other low floats with the same shape. Before bed she makes three changes:

  • She raises the top of her price filter from $3 to $12.
  • She drops the news requirement, because the runner's headline looked thin to her and it ran anyway.
  • She adds three names with the same float range and chart shape to tomorrow's watchlist.

Every change feels like homework. It is mourning dressed up as research.

The cost traders usually name, the next chase and the revenge click, happens in minutes and shows up on the statement. This one happens overnight and shows up on the watchlist. It rarely gets labeled FOMO, because by morning it's called the plan.

LOOKALIKES

Research has a test. Regret doesn't.

The next morning her wider scan is busier than usual, and three of the new names gap. She trades two of them on half size, just to feel in it. Both stop out.

In her journal the two trades go down as "trading my scan," and that's accurate. It just wasn't the scan she had written rules for a day earlier.

A setup has conditions. A lookalike has features. The $4 runner had a float, a chart shape, a price, a catalyst, a time of day and a tape. Overnight she copied the features that were easiest to see after the fact and deleted the condition her setup actually depended on. The features she copied show up in plenty of names in any given week, and many of those go nowhere.

A scanner edited the night of a miss stops looking for your setup and starts looking for the move you weren't in.

Studying the runner wasn't her mistake. Reviewing what you missed is how a lot of good setups get sharper. The difference is in what happens between the idea and the scanner:

  • Refinement starts with a written question, like "would my setup have caught this with a higher price cap?" It gets tested on every name the change would have caught over a past stretch, including the ones that went nowhere, and it goes live on a review day, attached to a setup already in writing.
  • Regret starts with one ticker, changes the scanner the same night, gets tested on nothing, and attaches to no written setup.

Run every match the new filter would have caught through it, the ones that went nowhere included, before the filter touches your live scan.

THE COMPOUND

Audit which filters you actually tested

Fast forward a few months. The $12 price cap came from the $4 runner, a tighter float limit from a squeeze she missed at lunch, and a premarket volume floor from a morning she was offline.

Each edit made sense the night she made it. Together they describe no setup she ever wrote down. That's one way results can look random while reads still feel sharp: she isn't running one strategy anymore, she's running several half strategies, each built around a trade she wasn't in.

Here is how to find out which of your filters belong to a tested setup and which came from a miss. Do it on a weekend, with your written rules open beside the scanner.

  1. List every filter on every scan you trade from, with its current value. A screenshot of each scan works.
  2. Next to each filter, quote the line in your written rules it serves. Not the setup's name, the actual line. If there's no line to quote, mark it.
  3. Ask what breaks without it. "If I deleted this filter, which written setup would stop finding its stocks?" If the answer is none, the filter isn't serving a setup.
  4. Ask what ticker it reminds you of. If a specific name or a specific missed morning comes to mind, check your journal or chat history around that date. A filter added the same week as a miss probably came from the miss.
  5. Ask whether it was tested. Can you point to trades or replays, logged before you started relying on the filter, that show your setup working with it in place? If not, it's written but untested.

Then sort every filter into one of three groups:

  • Written and tested: stays on the trade scan.
  • Written but untested: moves to a separate research scan until it has been tested.
  • Traced to a miss, or to nothing: moves to the research scan too, and comes back only once it's attached to a written setup and tested like anything else.

From then on, log every change with the date and a one-line reason, and make changes on a review day, not the night of a miss.

THE DEFENSE

Judge tomorrow's list against today's written rules

Before a name goes on the trade list, read it against your written criteria. Not "it looks like." Not "it reminds me of." The actual conditions, in order.

Those criteria aren't one checklist where ticking most of the boxes is good enough. They're three kinds of rule with different weights, and they run in this order:

  1. Disqualifiers. Any one of them takes the name off the list, whatever else it has going for it. Hers include a recent offering or an open shelf, and a spread too wide for her stop. Dilution Check reads a company's SEC filings for dilution risk, which is how she checks the first one.
  2. Mandatory conditions. Every one has to be there. Hers include news from that morning and a premarket level she can define. Missing one means it isn't her setup, however many features it shares with one.
  3. Preferences. A float in a range she likes, a cleaner chart. These rank names that already passed. They never rescue a name that didn't.

Run her two lookalikes through the rules she wrote before the miss and both fail at step two: no news that morning. Run them through the scanner she edited that night and both pass. Her rules never changed. The screen that feeds her the names did.

Names that fail go on a research list she can study as much as she likes, not on the list she trades from. Afterward, grade the day against the same rules. The MAKETZO journal grades how well you kept your rules, which is the question "trading my scan" never answers.

So does yesterday's miss write tomorrow's watchlist? Only if it gets to edit the scanner the same night, untested and attached to no written setup. Study the miss as much as you want, then make any idea it gives you earn its place like every other filter: written down, tested on the names that went nowhere as well as the one that ran, and checked against your rules before the first order.
