PREMARKET GAPS

Why Is a Stock Up Premarket With No News?

A small cap up premarket with nothing in the news column usually has a reason your feed does not carry: yesterday's after-hours move, a filing with no press release, a split your scanner miscounted, or another company's headline. When none of those turns up, you have not proven the move has no reason, but you are looking at momentum you cannot explain, so find out who can sell into it before you trade it.

Ludy D. · Fri Nov 20

No News Is a Search You Haven't Finished

7:40 a.m. on a hypothetical morning, built from a pattern small cap traders see often rather than from one real ticker: a stock that closed yesterday at $1.90 is trading at $2.85, up 50% on 900,000 shares, and the news column on your scanner is blank.

A blank column tells you what your feed picked up, not what happened. Plenty of reasons never go out on a news wire: a filing posted to EDGAR, a headline about a different company, a change in how your scanner counts. Give the search five minutes and a fixed order before you look at the chart again. The chart pulls you toward the buy button. The reason tells you whether anyone will still want the stock at 9:31.

Run every check against the clock. Note the time of the first heavy prints, then the time each possible reason became public. A reason that went public after the move started did not start it. It may bring in more buyers, but the first ones were reacting to something else, or simply to the price moving.

Put the morning on a timeline before you search:

  • 4:00 a.m. Extended-hours trading opens on Nasdaq.
  • 4:12 a.m. The first heavy prints: 60,000 shares in two minutes, with the price jumping from $1.95 to $2.40.
  • 6:00 a.m. EDGAR opens, and the filings held overnight start to appear.
  • 7:40 a.m. The stock reaches your scanner at $2.85.

Every check that follows gets compared with 4:12.

Check Yesterday's After-Hours Before This Morning's Feed

Scanners usually measure a premarket gap from yesterday's 4:00 p.m. close, so news that hit at 4:05 p.m. shows up as this morning's move.

Pull up yesterday's after-hours session on a one-minute chart. If the stock ran from 4:05 to 6:00 p.m. on a press release, the 7:40 gap is that headline being traded a second time, and the story may sit too far back in your feed to appear beside today's move.

A day-old headline changes who is in the stock. The after-hours buyers have had a night to sit on a profit and a premarket to take it. Compare yesterday's after-hours high with the price now. A stock that reached $3.20 after hours and trades at $2.85 at 7:40 is most likely being sold by the people who bought the headline, even while the scanner shows it up 50%. One that holds its after-hours high into the morning suggests new buyers are absorbing those sellers.

On the stock at $2.85, yesterday's after-hours session is flat: a few hundred shares near $1.90 and nothing after 5:00 p.m. The move began this morning.

Read the Filings That Never Reached the Wire

Most filings sent to EDGAR after 5:30 p.m. are dated the next business day and held until then, so they start to appear when EDGAR opens at 6:00 a.m., often with no press release beside them.

Search the company on EDGAR, sort by date, and read everything filed since yesterday's close. These forms can move a small cap without a headline:

  • 8-K. The item number tells you what happened before you read a word. Item 1.01 is a signed material agreement. Item 8.01 is any other event the company chose to report.
  • 6-K. Many companies based outside the U.S. file these instead of 8-Ks, and their news can land here first, or only here.
  • Form 4. An insider's change in holdings. Read the transaction code before you read anything into it: P is a purchase in the open market, S is a sale, and A or M is a grant or an option exercise.
  • Schedule 13D or 13G. A holder of more than 5% of the stock. One marked /A is an amendment and can report a stake that grew, shrank or fell under 5%, so check which way it moved.

Form 4 and Schedules 13D and 13G filed in the evening post that same night instead of waiting for morning, so read yesterday evening's filings too. Then compare each filing's time with 4:12. An 8-K that went public at 6:00 cannot explain a run that began two hours earlier.

A move nobody can explain raises a supply question first, and two forms answer most of it. A Form S-3 is a shelf registration: once effective, it lets the company sell stock later without registering each sale. A 424B5 is a prospectus supplement that puts part of that shelf to work, for an offering or to set up an at-the-market program.

The at-the-market program is the one traders reading only today's news miss. With one in place, the company does not need to announce anything this morning. Within the program's terms, its sales agent can sell shares into the market as buyers show up, with no press release for each sale, and the sales usually surface weeks later in the next quarterly report. How much it can sell depends on what is left in the program and, for a company with a small public float, a cap the SEC ties to that float. The filing that matters most this morning may not be the one posted at 6:00. It may be the 424B5 filed months ago that you never read.

Any filing reader has one blind spot. A deal priced overnight and not yet filed cannot appear anywhere, so check EDGAR again at 9:15.

Rule Out the Split and the Neighbors

Some gaps have nothing to do with the company's own news. Arithmetic creates some, and others are borrowed from another company.

  • A reverse split. If one took effect this morning, check that your scanner adjusted yesterday's close. A feed that compares a post-split price with a pre-split close turns a 1-for-10 split into a 900% gain nobody traded. Splits are announced days ahead, so the split is rarely in this morning's news.
  • A neighbor's headline. When one company in a hot theme posts real news, traders buy others in the same industry, or with the same buzzword in their description. Find the leader with the headline and check its time against 4:12. A follower has no reason of its own and depends on the leader, so when the leader stalls at the open, the follower tends to fade first.
  • A name. Rarely, the reason is a ticker that looks like a famous one. In March 2020 the SEC suspended trading in a little-known company that had not kept its filings current, after buyers mistook its ticker for Zoom Video's.

This morning's runner has no split, and its sector is quiet, with no peer up more than a few percent.

Can You Do Premarket Trading on a Gap With No Reason?

You can, and the order rules are the same as on any other morning. What changes is what holds the price up.

The mechanics of trading in premarket, from your broker's hours to which orders execute before 9:30 and what a stop order does at 8:00, apply here unchanged. What a no-reason gap lacks is a story for the next buyer. With a signed contract on EDGAR, a trader at 9:45 can read the filing and decide the company is worth more. Without a reason anyone can name, the main case for buying is that the price went up, and the move needs more buyers who see the same chart.

With no reason to lean on, most of the weight falls on the tape, and no single reading of it settles anything. Between 8:00 and 9:30, read four things together:

  • Volume against the earlier pushes. Compare each push with the 4:12 burst and the pushes since. A new high on lighter volume can mean buyers are thinning, or that sellers stepped away from the offer. The spread and the pullbacks tell you which.
  • The spread. A spread that widens as the price climbs often means the book is thinning, and your exit costs more by the minute. One that stays tight while it climbs usually means shares are trading in size on both sides.
  • The pullbacks. Watch where each dip stops. Dips that keep holding the same price show you where buyers are waiting. Dips that stop lower each time suggest buyers are backing off.
  • The levels. Yesterday's close, $2.40 where the first burst topped out, and the premarket high. Holding $2.40 and the high into the bell keeps the move intact. Losing $2.40 gives back the burst that started the morning.

A stock can have momentum without the trade deserving your money. Decide what you will do with a no-reason gap before 9:30, in writing, because at 9:31 it can be the biggest mover on your screen. A rule such as "no reason found, no trade until it holds the premarket high on volume after the open" is easy to write at 8:00 and hard to keep at the first spike.

The Five-Minute No-News Gap Check

Run the same five steps, in the same order, every time the news column is blank.

  • Trace the move. Put the first heavy prints on the clock, and check whether the run began in yesterday's after-hours or this morning.
  • Search for the catalyst. The wires, EDGAR since yesterday's close with last evening's Forms 4 and 13D or 13G included, and the leader in its theme.
  • Validate the gap. Rule out a reverse split, an unadjusted prior close and a ticker mix-up.
  • Check the supply. Is a shelf effective, is a program selling at the market, how recent was the last sale, and how long does the cash last?
  • Decide. Read volume against earlier pushes, the spread, the pullbacks and the levels, then trade it under your written rule or skip it.

Back to 7:40. The after-hours chart was flat, EDGAR showed nothing since the quarterly report, there was no split and no neighbor with news, and the company has sold stock twice this year with an open path to sell more. So why did it gap 50% on a blank news column? At 7:40, nobody can say what started it. You can say what did not: yesterday's news, an overnight filing, a split or a sector headline. The gap may keep running, and until a real reason shows up, a buy here is a bet on momentum with a financing risk sitting under it.
