You're not trading the float. You're trading the rotation.

Float rotation measures how hard a low float runner is trading, not who's trapped in it, and it can't justify the add you know breaks your rules.

Marcus T. · Mon Nov 23

Every add to a losing short comes with a reason. On a low float runner the reason usually sounds like analysis, and float rotation supplies some of the best ones.

Take a trade a lot of small cap traders will recognize, whether they made it themselves or watched it happen in chat. A runner with an estimated 4 million share float has traded 36 million shares by mid-morning, nine times the float. The trader reads that as exhausted supply: everyone who wanted the stock has already bought it. They short.

The stock doesn't care. Here is how the next half hour goes:

  • Up 30 cents: "High of day. That's resistance." First add.
  • Up another 40, right through the planned stop: "Volume's fading. It's tired." Second add.
  • Holding the highs: "Too many buyers trapped up here. It has to come in." Third add.

Every one of those lines sounds like a read on the tape. None of them was in the plan written before the first order, which was one entry and a stop above the morning high. By the third add the position is four times what the plan called for, and the stop has turned into a hope.

The stock keeps going, and that's how the account blows up. Not from a bad read on the float, but from a trader who kept finding new reasons to skip a rule they had already written. Knowing the word rotation didn't stop it. It supplied one of the excuses, which is why the number is worth getting exactly right.

WHAT ROTATION IS

Rotation counts trades, not owners

The arithmetic is simple. Divide the day's volume by the estimated float. A stock with a 3 million share float that has traded 30 million shares is at 10x rotation.

Here is everything 10x means: the volume so far equals ten times the float estimate. It does not mean every share changed hands ten times, that each share has had ten owners, or that ten fresh cost bases are stacked on the chart. Rotation counts trades, not owners.

The same shares can trade over and over. A scalper who buys and sells the same 10,000 shares fifty times adds a million shares to the day's volume and never holds more than 10,000 at once. Market makers and fast traders turn over the same inventory all session, while some holders never sell a share. Volume can be enormous while much of the float sits still.

Think of rotation as a turnstile count. It tells you how many times the gate turned. It can't tell you how many people are inside, when they came in, or who is standing by the exit.

The float itself deserves the same suspicion. Data providers calculate it differently and update it on different schedules, so two screens can show two floats for the same stock, and some figures trail the company's filings by weeks or more. An offering, a note conversion, warrant exercises or a reverse split can put the number on your screen out of date overnight. If the real float is double what you see, your 10x is a 5x.

WHAT IT CAN'T TELL YOU

Trapped buyers are a theory

The popular read on heavy rotation goes like this. Lots of volume at higher prices means lots of buyers paid up, some of them are underwater, and they will sell when price comes back to their entry. That can happen, and it's a reasonable hypothesis about a crowded stock. It is not something the volume shows you.

Split what you know from what you're guessing:

  • What you can observe: the day's volume, an estimate of the float, the ratio between them, how fast volume is printing, and price.
  • What you're inferring: who still holds the stock, what they paid, whether they will sell, and at what price.

A busy turnstile tells you the gate has been turning. It doesn't tell you whether the crowd inside is heading for the exit.

Now go back to the short. Supply is exhausted was an inference. Trapped buyers will sell into this was an inference. Each was a plausible story about people the trader couldn't see, and each arrived right when the trader needed a reason to add.

Every number on a low float runner can become a reason to add to a loser. The more you know, the smarter the reason sounds.

TWO DIFFERENT QUESTIONS

Relative volume and rotation don't compete

Relative volume compares today's volume to the stock's own average, and answers one question: is participation unusual for this stock? Rotation compares today's volume to the estimated float, and answers a different one: how much has traded relative to the supply that's supposed to be tradable?

The two can disagree and both be right. A stock that averages 500,000 shares a day with a 10 million share float can trade 10 million shares and sit at 20x relative volume and 1x rotation. Something unusual is clearly happening, and the volume so far equals one float's worth.

Neither number tells you who owns the stock, which way it goes next, or whether your entry is any good. Read both as descriptions of activity. Don't ask either one for permission.

WHAT TO WATCH LIVE

Four steps before every order on a runner

None of this predicts price. What it does is slow you down long enough to see which part of your reason is a measurement and which part is a story. Run it before the first entry and again before any add.

  1. Check the float before you trust it. Compare it across two sources, and if they disagree by a lot, assume neither is precise. Look for recent offerings, shelf registrations, warrants or convertible notes, any of which can mean the share count is changing. Dilution Check reads a company's SEC filings for dilution risk, so you know whether more shares may be coming before you lean on any float number.
  2. Do the division and write it down. Volume so far divided by the estimated float, rounded, with the time beside it. Note how long the last float's worth of volume took. It's arithmetic, not an indicator.
  3. Write one line about what the number can't tell you. "This says how much has traded. It doesn't say who holds it, where they sell, or where price goes." If your reason for the trade includes trapped, exhausted or has to, you're trading a hypothesis, so call it one.
  4. Hold the order against the rules you wrote before the open. Your max loss on the trade. Whether you add to a loser, yes or no. Where you're out. Your daily limit. If the order breaks any of them it doesn't go in, however good the rotation story sounds.

Step four is the one the trader in the short skipped, three times. The rule existed, and each add came with a fresh reason it didn't apply this time. If you hit a situation your rules don't cover, write that rule after the close, not in the middle of the trade.

WHAT YOU'RE ACTUALLY TRADING

So are you trading the rotation?

The short didn't fail because the trader misread nine rotations. It failed because one entry became four, each justified on the spot by a number that measured activity, not ownership. A sharper rotation read wouldn't have saved that account. Keeping the rule would have made it one stopped-out loss.

So, are you trading the rotation? Partly. The float on your screen is an estimate that may be out of date, and the rotation is the trading happening in front of you, so the headline holds that far.

But rotation measures trading activity relative to the float, and nothing more. It can't tell you who owns the shares, where resistance is, or what the right trade is. That decision is still yours, and the version you can trust is the one you wrote down before the stock started moving.
