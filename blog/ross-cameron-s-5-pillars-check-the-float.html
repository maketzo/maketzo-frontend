STOCK SELECTION

Ross Cameron's 5 Pillars: Check the Float, Then the Filings

Ross Cameron's five pillars of stock selection are price, percent change on the day, relative volume, a news catalyst and float, and a stock has to meet all five before he calls it an A quality stock. The first four measure demand and the fifth measures supply, and supply is the only pillar that can change while the stock is running.

Marcus T. · Sat Nov 14

How Ross Cameron Found His Five Pillars

Write down what your best trades had in common, and you have the start of a stock screen. That is how Ross Cameron built his.

He started with a small account, and in his book How to Day Trade: The Plain Truth he explains why he would not hold a stock overnight. News of a "secondary offering" could arrive after the close, and the stock "could literally drop 40 or 50 percent overnight." So he traded only what was already moving that day and studied which of those trades paid.

The list came out of his own results, one discovery at a time. The stocks that worked were up at least 10 percent on the day, trading on far more volume than usual, moving on breaking news and priced where small accounts trade. Then came the fifth: the biggest movers "typically had a very limited number of shares available to trade."

He sorted the five himself. The first four "were all based on the criteria that create a high level of demand. The fifth is based on supply."

Keep that split in your head at the open. Demand tells you a crowd is buying. Supply tells you how many shares that crowd has to fight over.

The Four Pillars That Measure Demand

Demand is what your scanner shows at 9:30. Here is each pillar as his 2025 Small Account Tool Kit states it, and how to read it in the first minutes of the day.

  • Price. $1 to $20 in the kit, $2 to $20 in his book. His reason is practical: "If the stock is too expensive, I won't be able to afford enough shares to make the trade worth it." That range is also the most popular with retail traders on small accounts, and the crowd is the point.
  • Percent change. Up at least 10 percent on the day. "I'll never buy a stock that's not already moving," he writes. The one exception he names is a continuation: a stock that made a big move the day before and is still holding those prices.
  • Relative volume. At least five times its normal volume. Volume that heavy means something happened, and it means other traders found the same name you did.
  • News. "There must be a headline that justifies why the stock is moving." A move with no news can still work, but in his words it carries "more risk of a sudden drop."

The news pillar is the one traders skip when a chart looks good. Make it a hard test: if you cannot say why the stock is up in one sentence, it has not met the pillar, however strong the chart looks.

The Fifth Pillar Is Supply

Float decides how far a crowd can push a stock, because it caps how many shares there are to buy.

Float is the number of shares free to trade, and his day trading guide puts it in four words: "The float is the supply." The kit prefers floats under 20 million shares, and lower is better as long as the stock meets the other four. His reasoning is supply and demand. Heavy buying into a small supply produces the imbalances that "create 50% - 100%+ intraday moves."

The same guide calls the float "one of the first things we check," and it says what can change it. A buyback shrinks the float. And "a company can increase their float by selling more shares on the open market through offerings. Selling more shares will increase the float and decrease the value of the shares."

That sentence is the whole lesson. The float on your scanner is a count of the shares trading right now. It cannot tell you how many more the company is able to add before the close.

Can the Company Sell Stock Into the Run?

A company with a way to sell stock and not much cash has every reason to sell into a spike in its own share price. The new shares that matter to a low float trade arrive three ways.

  • A shelf registration. An effective shelf registers stock the company can sell later, so a sale does not wait on a fresh review.
  • An at-the-market program. A sales agreement that lets the company sell shares straight into the market at the going price, a little at a time, on any trading day, including the day its stock runs.
  • Warrants and convertible notes. Paper that can turn into stock without the company filing anything new.

Then check how long the money lasts. Cash from the last quarterly report, set against what the company spends to run, tells you whether it needs to raise soon. An open selling channel, sales in the last few months and a short cash runway together describe a company in the habit of selling its stock.

Three habits make the read worth having:

  • Unknown never means no. When a filing cannot confirm something, treat it as open, not as zero.

All Five, or No Trade

A stock that fails one pillar stays off your screen, even at 9:41 when it is up 60 percent and you want in anyway.

Ross sets the bar himself: "I need a stock to meet all of these criteria to be considered an A quality stock for day trading." Writing that rule down is easy. Ross began by refusing to hold overnight because an offering could hit a stock while he slept. The same risk runs during the day, through a selling program that does not announce each sale. So what are Ross Cameron's five pillars? Price, percent change, relative volume, news and float: four checks on demand and one on supply. Run all five on your scanner, then ask the fifth pillar its second question in the filings: can this company sell more stock today?
