How Does Day Trading Work? Follow One Small Cap Trade From Scan to Close

How does day trading work? You buy and sell the same stock inside one session, finish the day with no open trades, and earn only what your winners pay after your losers and your costs.

One Day Trade, Start to Finish

It is 8:05 a.m. and a made-up small cap that closed at $2.10 is trading at $3.30 on overnight news. Here is what a day trader does with it, in order.

  • Find the reason. Read the headline or the filing itself. If you cannot say why the stock is up in one sentence, there is nothing to plan around.
  • Check who can sell. Type the ticker into Dilution Check. It reads the company's SEC filings for dilution risk and shows whether the company has an open way to sell new shares into this price.
  • Write the plan before the bell. Buy only if it clears $3.50 on real volume. Wrong if it falls back under $3.30. First exit near $3.80. All three prices get written down at 8:40, while you are calm.
  • Send the order. At 9:34 the stock trades through $3.50. You send a limit just through the offer and fill at $3.52.
  • Manage it. Price reaches $3.80 and you sell into buyers. Had the stock frozen on the way up, a halt would have left you with no exit until it reopened.
  • Get out. You are flat by 9:52, and nothing goes home with you.
  • Record it. Entry, exit, what it paid per share and whether you followed the plan.

The winning version made 28 cents a share before costs. In the losing version, the stock falls under $3.30, your stop order fills at $3.27 and the trade loses 25 cents. Say costs come to 1 cent a share for the round trip. A win nets 27 cents and a loss costs 26. That is the whole business: the same sequence, repeated, with a result that only means something across hundreds of trades.

Day Trading Rules in 2026

Ask your broker which rulebook it runs today, because the answer changed in June 2026.

For years, four day trades in five business days in a margin account labeled you a pattern day trader and required $25,000 in equity. On June 4, 2026, FINRA replaced that with intraday margin requirements, and there is no longer a designation based on counting trades. Your firm now checks during the day whether your equity covers what you are trading. Some firms can stay on the old rules through October 20, 2027, so yours may still be counting.

  • Margin account. Leverage takes at least $2,000 in equity. With less, you trade only the cash you have. Maintenance margin of 25% on long margin-eligible stock applies throughout the day, firms can demand more, and repeated unmet deficits can restrict an account for up to 90 days.
  • Cash account. You trade only what has settled, and stock settles one business day after the trade. Sell Monday morning and that money is unsettled until Tuesday. Buy with it Monday afternoon and sell before Tuesday, and you have a good faith violation, which can restrict the account. Buying and selling before paying at all is freeriding, and it can freeze a cash account for 90 days.
  • Wash sales. Sell at a loss and buy the same stock within 30 days before or after, and the loss is disallowed for now and added to the cost of the new shares. It is deferred and not erased. A day trader who keeps returning to the same name runs into this constantly, and a December loss you rebuy in January can land in the next tax year.

That is general information and not tax or legal advice, so confirm the specifics with your broker or a qualified professional.

How Your Order Becomes a Fill

The price on your screen is a quote for a limited number of shares, and your fill is whatever is left when the order arrives.

The SEC says it plainly: "Price quotes are only for a specific number of shares." Your broker may send an order to an exchange, an electronic network or a market maker, or fill it from its own inventory, and some platforms let you pick the route. Either way, the price can change before the order gets there.

  • Market order. Buys whatever is offered until it is full. Reasonable in a deep stock and reckless in a thin one.
  • Limit order. Your price or better, with no promise that it fills while the stock runs away.
  • Marketable limit. A limit set at or just through the offer, for a fast fill with a ceiling on what you pay.
  • Stop-market. Becomes a market order once triggered. You get out, and nobody can tell you at what price.
  • Stop-limit. Becomes a limit order once triggered, and in a fast drop it can leave you in the trade.

Market and stop-market guarantee the execution and never the price. Limit and stop-limit guarantee the price and never the execution. Choose which one you need before you send, and work out what the spread and fees take with the break-even math for scalping, which applies to any day trade.

What a Trading Day Looks Like

The day has a shape, and a plan that ignores the clock pays for it.

  • 4:00 a.m. Nasdaq's extended session begins. Premarket trading runs on thin quotes, and many retail brokers open later.
  • 6:00 a.m. EDGAR opens, and most filings sent after 5:30 p.m. the night before appear.
  • 9:30 a.m. The heaviest volume, the biggest moves and the most noise arrive together. Price bands begin, and a stock that runs too fast can freeze. MAKETZO's Halt Clock estimates when a volatility-halted stock is likely to reopen.
  • Midday. Volume thins and price chops. The setups get worse and the urge to trade does not, so this is when to trade less.
  • The last hour. Volume and volatility come back as the day's trades get closed.
  • 3:50 p.m. A volatility pause whose auction would end after this point goes straight to the closing auction.
  • 4:00 p.m. The bell. Anything still open is no longer a day trade. It is an overnight bet on a stock that can gap against you before you can act.

How Day Traders Make or Lose Money

Your results come down to one line: win rate times average win, minus loss rate times average loss.

Run the morning's trade through it. Win half the time at 27 cents and lose half the time at 26, and you make half a cent a share per trade. Let the average loss grow by 3 cents, to 29, and the same trader loses a penny a share per trade. Three cents of hesitation on the exit is the difference between a thin edge and a slow bleed.

Every method produces losing streaks, and your job is to still be trading when one ends. A 50% drawdown needs a 100% gain just to get back to even, and that is why survival comes before profit.

FINRA requires firms that promote day trading to give customers a risk statement, and it does not soften the point: "You should be prepared to lose all of the funds that you use for day trading." Take that sentence literally.

Why Knowing How It Works Is Not Enough

You already know not to chase. The trade you regret at noon is usually one you knew better than to take at 9:40.

Rules get written by the calm part of your brain, and orders get sent by the part that wants relief, revenge or to be right. More information does not close that gap. Rehearsal and structure do.

Rehearse first. Trader Type drops you onto a simulated small cap tape for two minutes, free and with no login. Trade Replay lets you enter the buys and sells you would have made on any past chart and see whether they paid.

Then write rules you can answer yes or no.

  • I can name why this stock is up in one sentence.
  • My entry, my exit and the price that proves me wrong are written down before I send.
  • I know which account rules my broker runs.
  • I am flat by the close.
  • After a broken rule, I reset before the next order.

Common Questions

How much money do you need to start day trading?

No single number fits. A cash account has no day trading minimum, and you trade only what has settled. Using leverage takes at least $2,000 in equity under FINRA's intraday margin requirements, and some firms can keep the old $25,000 rule through October 20, 2027, so ask yours. Past the rules, the real question is whether a normal losing streak would leave you able to keep trading. Money you cannot afford to lose does not belong in the account.

Can you make a living day trading?

Very few people do. A study of day traders in Taiwan from 1992 to 2006 found that less than 1% could predictably and reliably earn positive abnormal returns net of fees. That market is not a U.S. small cap tape, and the conclusion still belongs in your plan. Treat day trading income as unproven until your own record shows a net profit after every cost, across hundreds of trades.

How do beginners start day trading?

Learn the account rules first, then pick one setup and learn it well. Rehearse it in a simulator, write rules you can answer yes or no, and keep a record of every trade from the first one, including whether you followed the plan. Taking fewer trades on fewer names costs you less while you learn, and the record will tell you more than another hour of watching other people trade.
